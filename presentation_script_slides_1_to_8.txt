SLIDE 3 — Topic Review: PPG & Adaptive Interference Cancellation (~0:50 | ~125 words)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Let's recap the physical setup. A wrist-worn PPG sensor shines green LED light
into the skin and measures reflected intensity with a photodiode. As blood
volume pulses with each heartbeat, light absorption changes, revealing the heart
rate. During exercise, however, wrist movement shifts the optical path and
injects strong motion artifacts directly inside the cardiac frequency band.

To cancel this interference, we use the adaptive noise cancellation scheme on
the right. The PPG channel is the primary input: it holds the clean pulse plus
motion noise. The three-axis accelerometer provides reference signals that
correlate with wrist movement, not with the heartbeat. Our adaptive filter—now
RLS—estimates the motion noise from the accelerometer and subtracts it from the
PPG, leaving the cleaned cardiac signal as the error output e[n].

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 4 — Pipeline: How the Code Works (~1:10 | ~180 words)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Every 8-second window contains 1,000 samples at 125 Hz and is processed
completely independently—no filter state or weights carry over from one window
to the next. The pipeline has five steps:

1. Preprocessing: Both PPG channels and all three accelerometer axes pass
   through a zero-phase 4th-order Butterworth bandpass filter from 0.5 to 4 Hz
   (30 to 240 BPM), removing baseline drift and out-of-band noise. Each channel
   is then normalized to zero mean and unit variance.
2. Regressors: We stack the past M delayed samples of the three accelerometer
   axes into a regressor matrix U of size N by 3M, zero-padded before n = 0.
3. Parallel Filtering: Because the dataset has two wrist PPG sensors, we filter
   PPG 1 and PPG 2 in parallel against the accelerometer using the same algorithm
   and hyperparameters.
4. SNR-Weighted Late Fusion: We combine both cleaned outputs using weights
   proportional to each branch's in-band spectral SNR, automatically trusting
   the cleaner sensor.
5. BPM Estimation: Finally, we take the dominant peak of a zero-padded
   periodogram in 0.5 to 4 Hz. We also tested Elgendi's peak detector in our
   search loop, and the periodogram consistently won.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 5 — Optimizing RLS: LMS vs. RLS (~0:50 | ~130 words)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Why move from LMS to RLS? Look at the difference between the two methods.

LMS—and its Project 1 variants NLMS and VS-LMS—updates its weights using only
the instantaneous gradient at the current sample. That is simple and cheap,
O(L) operations per sample where L = 3M. However, when the input lags are
strongly correlated, gradient descent zig-zags and converges slowly. Since each
window is only 8 seconds long and starts cold from zero weights, slow
convergence hurts accuracy.

RLS instead solves a deterministic weighted least-squares problem at every
sample, choosing the weights that minimize the accumulated squared error over
all samples seen so far in the window. Using the inverse correlation matrix
P(n), RLS whitens the input directions and achieves much faster, Newton-like
convergence—at a higher cost of O(L^2) per sample.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 6 — How RLS Works: Forgetting Factor & P(n) (~0:55 | ~140 words)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
More specifically, RLS minimizes an exponentially weighted sum of squared
errors: the most recent sample has weight 1, and older samples decay as lambda,
lambda^2, lambda^3, and so on.

The forgetting factor lambda sets the memory of the filter. When lambda is
close to 1, RLS averages over almost the whole 8-second window, giving stable,
low-variance weights. When lambda is smaller, RLS forgets older samples faster
and tracks sudden changes in motion better, at the price of noisier weights.

The matrix P(n) recursively tracks the inverse of the weighted input correlation
matrix Phi(n). Crucially, Phi(n) and P(n) depend only on the accelerometer
reference a(n), not on the PPG signal. By multiplying the error by P(n) * a(n),
RLS rescales each direction according to how much motion energy occurred along
it, eliminating the zig-zagging that slows down LMS.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 7 — Parameters We Study (~0:45 | ~115 words)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
In standard textbook RLS, there are three parameters to optimize via 5-fold
subject-wise Group K-Fold cross-validation:

1. M: the filter order per accelerometer axis (total weights L = 3M).
2. Lambda: the forgetting factor controlling effective memory.
3. Delta: the initial regularization setting P(0) = (1/delta)*I.

However, when we tested naive textbook RLS, it barely matched Project 1's
VS-LMS and hit severe numerical issues: our Bayesian TPE optimizer pushed
lambda right up against its upper limit (0.9999) and delta down against its
lower limit (0.01). Fixing why that happened led directly to the four
improvements on Slide 8.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 8 — Additional Pipeline Improvements Relative to Project 1 (~4:40 | ~720 words)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Let's go carefully through the four improvements on Slide 8, because they fix
bottlenecks we saw in Project 1 and explain why our final comparison is fair.

Improvement 1: Dilated Taps (delay_step = D).
In Project 1, our regressor took consecutive samples, D = 1, at 125 Hz. Why is
that bad? Because our signals are bandpass-filtered to 0.5–4 Hz, they are
oversampled more than 15 times—consecutive samples are over 98.5% identical.
That extreme correlation makes the input correlation matrix nearly singular: a
few eigenvalues are large, while most are almost zero (around 10^-7 or smaller),
giving a condition number above 10^6. For LMS, gradient descent stalls along
those tiny-eigenvalue directions. For RLS, it is even worse: P(n) inverts that
correlation matrix, so dividing by near-zero eigenvalues blows up numerical
noise into huge weight errors. At the same time, 15 consecutive samples only
span 112 milliseconds—shorter than a quarter cycle of a running stride. By
spacing taps by D = 4 samples (32 milliseconds apart) and using M = 7 taps,
adjacent taps are genuinely distinct, the condition number drops dramatically,
we need only 21 weights instead of 45, and our filter spans nearly 200 milliseconds.

Improvement 2: Huber Robust Innovation Clipping (huber_c).
In Project 1, we saw severe outliers like Window #481, where a sudden impact
caused an enormous error spike. In adaptive filtering, "innovation" means the
prediction error e[n] = d[n] - m_hat[n]—the part of the PPG not predicted from
the accelerometer. Importantly, we do NOT clip the output PPG signal: unclipped
e[n] goes straight to the periodogram. Instead, in the code, we track a running
standard deviation of the error and compute a clipped copy, e_upd, capped at
+/- 2.5 standard deviations, using only e_upd to update the weights.

You might ask: doesn't the spike stay in lambda's memory, or doesn't clipping
slow down real adaptation? The answer to both is no. First, the correlation
matrix P(n) only stores the accelerometer a(n), never the PPG d(n). Past PPG
information lives only in the weight vector w(n)—so clipping e_upd stops the
spike from ever entering w(n) in the first place, preventing a mid-window shock
from throwing the weights off course for hundreds of samples. Second, if motion
genuinely changes, the error persists across many consecutive samples: every
step still pulls w(n) toward the new regime, P(n) and k(n) stay unclipped to
track the new accelerometer energy, and the running error scale adapts upward.
It simply ignores single-sample heavy-tailed shocks.

Improvement 3: Scaled Initialization, Diagonal Loading, and Effective Memory.
In textbook RLS, two issues forced TPE to pin lambda -> 1 and delta -> 0.01.
First, textbook regularization enters the cost as delta * lambda^(n+1) * I,
which fades away exponentially when lambda < 1. Along any unexcited null-space
direction of the accelerometer—where a^T(n)*v is nearly zero—the update
P(n+1) = (1/lambda)*(P(n) - k(n)*a^T(n)*P(n)) subtracts nothing and simply
multiplies that eigenvalue by 1/lambda > 1 at every sample! So when lambda < 1,
null-space entries of P(n) blow up exponentially as (1/lambda)^n. Second, even
though the heartbeat isn't in the accelerometer, over a very short effective
memory (like 50 samples = 0.4 s at lambda = 0.98), two narrowband 0.5–4 Hz
waves overlap purely by chance—so with 45 weights, RLS can cancel part of the
pulse. To avoid both failures, TPE pinned lambda -> 1; and once lambda ~ 1,
initial delta never decays, forcing TPE to pin delta -> 0.01.

We fixed all three together: (a) we scale P(0) = 1 / (delta * 3M) * I by the
expected input power 3M so delta has the same meaning for any M; (b) we apply
persistent diagonal loading (diag_load), gently shrinking P(n) back toward
P(0)*I at every step so null-space growth is damped and bounded without forcing
lambda = 1; and (c) we optimize effective memory N_eff = 1/(1 - lambda) on a log
scale instead of lambda linearly, yielding an interior optimum (N_eff = 1409,
delta = 5.11).

Improvement 4: Fair Comparison ("Option B").
Dilated taps and Huber clipping help the whole pipeline, not just RLS. So we
applied the exact same delay_step = 4 and huber_c = 2.50 to LMS, NLMS, and
VS-LMS—while keeping their Project 1 optimal step sizes and filter orders fixed,
without retraining them.

That explains why Standard LMS suddenly beats VS-LMS on test (13.09 vs. 13.94
BPM AAE), even though VS-LMS still wins in cross-validation (4.20 vs. 5.18 BPM).
Once D = 4 decorrelates the input and Huber clipping removes spikes, Standard
LMS—with 33 weights and a tiny fixed step (mu = 0.0007)—is very stable. Meanwhile,
VS-LMS was not re-tuned for D = 4; its five D = 1 parameters and error-driven
step-size boost over-react on harder unseen test subjects. Next, let's see how
RLS performs against this strengthened baseline.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A. Core RLS Theory (From Cost Function to Recursive Update)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Objective Function:
   At sample n, RLS finds the single weight vector w that minimizes the
   exponentially weighted deterministic least-squares cost over all samples
   i = 0, ..., n in the current window:

       J[n] = sum_{i=0}^{n} lambda^{n - i} (d[i] - w^T a[i])^2
              + delta * lambda^{n + 1} ||w||^2

   Here:
   - d[i] is the preprocessed PPG signal,
   - a[i] is the 3M-dimensional accelerometer regressor vector,
   - lambda in (0, 1] is the forgetting factor (effective memory N_eff = 1/(1 - lambda)),
   - delta > 0 is the initial ridge regularization weight.

2. Normal Equations:
   Setting grad_w J[n] = 0 gives the linear system:

       Phi[n] * w[n + 1] = z[n]

   where:
       Phi[n] = sum_{i=0}^{n} lambda^{n - i} a[i] a^T[i]  +  delta * lambda^{n + 1} I
       z[n]   = sum_{i=0}^{n} lambda^{n - i} a[i] d[i]

   Notice that Phi[n] depends ONLY on the accelerometer vectors a[i], whereas
   d[i] enters ONLY through z[n] (and recursively through w[n]).

3. Recursive Matrix Inversion Lemma (Woodbury Identity):
   Inverting Phi[n] directly takes O(L^3) per sample. Defining P[n] = Phi^{-1}[n - 1]
   with P[0] = (1 / delta) * I gives the exact O(L^2) recursion:
     - A priori error (innovation):  e[n] = d[n] - w^T[n] a[n]
     - Gain vector:                  k[n] = P[n] a[n] / (lambda + a^T[n] P[n] a[n])
     - Weight update:                w[n + 1] = w[n] + k[n] * e[n]
     - Inverse correlation update:   P[n + 1] = (1 / lambda) * (P[n] - k[n] a^T[n] P[n])

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
B. Where Naive RLS Improves Over LMS vs. Where It Fails
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Where RLS Improves Over LMS:
  LMS takes steps along the raw gradient e[n] * a[n]. If a[n] has very uneven
  energy across eigendirections, LMS converges rapidly in high-energy modes and
  crawls in low-energy modes. RLS multiplies by P[n] = Phi^{-1}, which acts like
  a Newton pre-conditioner: it equalizes step sizes across all excited
  eigendirections and converges in ~2L samples instead of hundreds of samples.

- Where Naive RLS Fails on Isolated 8-Second Bandlimited PPG Windows:
  1) Extreme Collinearity (D = 1 at 125 Hz in 0.5–4 Hz):
     The input occupies only 5.6% of the Nyquist band, so consecutive samples
     are >98.5% correlated (condition number > 10^6). Inverting nearly singular
     Phi[n] amplifies noise in weak eigenmodes. (Fixed by delay_step = D.)
  2) Null-Space Blowup When lambda < 1:
     See Section C below. (Fixed by diag_load + N_eff.)
  3) Finite-Window Heartbeat Cancellation:
     Over short effective memory N_eff (e.g., 50 samples = 0.4 s at lambda = 0.98),
     the uncorrelated heartbeat s[n] and accelerometer a[n] share the same
     0.5–4 Hz band and exhibit non-zero sample cross-correlation by chance.
     With L = 45 free weights, RLS uses excess degrees of freedom to fit and
     cancel s[n]. (Fixed by fewer taps M=7 + log-scale N_eff ~ 1409.)
  4) Quadratic Sensitivity to Impulsive Motion Shocks:
     Because J[n] is an L2 squared loss, a sudden sensor detachment spike
     creates a massive e[n] that kicks w[n + 1] far from the true motion path,
     and once P[n] has shrunk (O(1/n)), recovery takes hundreds of samples.
     (Fixed by huber_c; note that on completely saturated windows where the
     cardiac peak disappears—e.g. Window #481—no linear filter can recover BPM.)

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
C. Diagonal Loading & Why lambda < 1 Blows Up Null-Space Directions
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. How Regularization Was NOT Equal Before (Textbook vs. Persistent):
   In textbook RLS, the regularization term in Phi[n] is delta * lambda^{n+1} * I.
   Because lambda < 1, that diagonal loading decays to ZERO exponentially as n
   grows (e.g., at lambda = 0.98 and n = 300, 0.98^300 = 0.0023). It is NOT
   persistent—it only exists at the very start of the window.

2. Why Unexcited Null-Space Directions Blow Up When lambda < 1:
   Let v_q be an eigenvector in the unexcited null space of the accelerometer
   where a^T[n] v_q ~= 0. Look at the textbook P[n] recursion:
       P[n + 1] v_q = (1 / lambda) * (P[n] v_q - k[n] * (a^T[n] P[n] v_q))
   Because a^T[n] v_q ~= 0 (since P[n] v_q aligns with v_q), the subtracted
   rank-1 term is zero, leaving:
       v_q^T P[n + 1] v_q  ~=  (1 / lambda) * (v_q^T P[n] v_q)
                           ~=  (1 / delta) * (1 / lambda)^n  ->  infinity!
   Because 1/lambda > 1, every single sample inflates unexcited eigenvalues of P[n]
   exponentially. That forces TPE to push lambda -> 1.0 (to keep 1/lambda ~= 1).
   And once lambda ~= 1, initial delta * I never decays, so large delta
   permanently shrinks weights toward zero—forcing TPE to push delta -> 0.01.

3. What Persistent Diagonal Loading (diag_load) Does:
   "Diagonal loading" means continuously adding a positive floor to the diagonal
   of Phi[n] (or shrinking P[n] back toward its prior P_0 * I = 1/(delta*3M) * I
   on every step):
       P[n + 1] = (1 - tau) * P_tilde[n + 1] + tau * P_0 * I
   with tau = (1 - lambda) * diag_load. Along an unexcited direction v_q, the
   growth factor changes from 1/lambda to (1 - tau)/lambda (reaching exact
   neutrality = 1.0 when diag_load = 1.0, and damping growth while re-anchoring
   the diagonal toward P_0 for 0 < diag_load < 1). In our ablation on 300
   training windows, removing diag_load worsened median error from 0.84 to 0.99 BPM.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
D. How Huber Innovation Clipping Changes RLS Theory & Why Adaptation Still Works
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Does the Bad Spike Stay in lambda's Memory?
   NO. Remember the two parts of RLS:
   - Phi[n] (and P[n] = Phi^{-1}) accumulates ONLY the accelerometer input a[i]:
         Phi[n] = sum lambda^{n-i} a[i] a^T[i] + ...
     The PPG spike d[n_0] is NEVER inside P[n] or lambda's correlation memory!
   - Target PPG information enters ONLY through the weight update:
         w[n + 1] = w[n] + k[n] * e_upd[n]
     Without clipping, a 20*sigma spike in d[n_0] creates a huge jump in w[n_0+1],
     and THAT corrupted weight jump persists in w[n] for hundreds of samples.
     With Huber clipping (e_upd = clip(e[n], +/- 2.5 * sigma_e[n])), the spike is
     blocked BEFORE entering w[n], corresponding theoretically to replacing L2
     quadratic loss with a robust Huber M-estimator loss (quadratic for small
     errors, linear/bounded gradient for outliers). Meanwhile, unclipped e[n] is
     still returned as the output signal.

2. Why Doesn't Clipping Degrade Genuine Adaptation?
   - P[n] and the Kalman gain k[n] are NEVER clipped, so sudden changes in
     accelerometer energy a[n] immediately update the gain geometry.
   - A true change in the motion transfer function produces a SUSTAINED error
     over many consecutive samples (not a single isolated spike). Even when
     capped at +/- 2.5*sigma_e, every sample pulls w[n] in the right direction,
     and the running scale tracker (sigma_e^2 = 0.98*sigma_e^2 + 0.02*e_upd^2)
     gradually expands to match sustained higher error.
   - Trade-off: If a legitimate, huge step-change in motion path occurs mid-window,
     Huber clipping slightly slows the first few samples of re-convergence
     (trading a tiny bit of step-response speed for immunity against outliers).
     In our 300-window training ablation, RLS with and without Huber both scored
     3.57 BPM AAE, showing it costs nothing on typical windows while protecting
     against heavy-tailed test spikes.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- NO. Under Project 2's Option B protocol, only RLS was optimized on the new
  pipeline. Standard LMS, NLMS, and VS-LMS kept their exact Project 1 optimal
  step sizes and filter orders (tuned in Project 1 for D = 1 without Huber),
  and were evaluated with RLS's delay_step = 4 and huber_c = 2.50149.
- Consequently, VS-LMS's five reactive parameters (M=18, mu_max=0.06558, etc.)
